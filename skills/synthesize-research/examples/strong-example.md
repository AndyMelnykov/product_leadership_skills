# Strong Example: Onboarding Confusion Synthesis

**Input**: 5 customer interviews with enterprise admins reporting onboarding confusion, plus a 200-response NPS survey whose free-text write-ins contain no onboarding complaints.

## Key Findings

**Finding 1: Enterprise admins get stuck on the permissions-setup step of onboarding**

- **Evidence**: "I had no idea which of the nine roles to assign myself" (Enterprise admin, 200-person team). "I set up three test accounts before I understood the role model" (Enterprise admin, 500-person team). 5 of 5 interviewed admins independently described this same step as where they got stuck.
- **Frequency**: 5/5 interview participants, enterprise-admin segment only.
- **Impact**: High — participants described re-attempting setup, contacting support, or asking a colleague for help before proceeding.
- **Confidence**: Medium. Strong within the enterprise-admin segment interviewed, but see the contradiction below before generalizing to the full user base.

**Contradiction with survey data**: A concurrent 200-response NPS survey's free-text write-ins contain zero mentions of onboarding friction. This is not necessarily a conflict — the survey population skews toward smaller, self-serve accounts, a segment not represented in the 5 interviews (all sourced from enterprise accounts via customer success referrals). The two sources may describe different populations rather than disagree about the same one. It's also possible that NPS write-ins, being unprompted, simply don't surface a friction point respondents didn't think to volunteer.

## Open Questions

- Does self-serve/SMB onboarding show the same permissions-step confusion, or is this specific to enterprise accounts?
- Would a targeted onboarding-satisfaction question elicit different write-in behavior than open-ended NPS comments?

## Recommended Next Step

Run a short, targeted survey of the enterprise-admin segment specifically (or add an onboarding-specific question to the next NPS cycle) before committing engineering time to a fix — the current evidence is strong enough to justify that follow-up, not yet strong enough to justify a redesign.

---

This finding is offered at medium confidence, scoped explicitly to the segment it was observed in, with the contradicting source named and a concrete plan to resolve the disagreement rather than picking a side.
