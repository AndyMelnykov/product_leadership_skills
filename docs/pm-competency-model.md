# PM Competency Model

This document explains the product management competency model used in this repository. It is based on the PM competency model by Ravi Mehta (Product Competency Toolkit, v1.0.1 — guide, one-pager, and self-assessment worksheets).

## Why the model matters

The model helps people grow and align expectations across the organization. It creates a shared language for:

- coaching and development
- performance review and calibration
- hiring and interview evaluation

The idea is to make competencies visible, observable, and consistent. Once teams agree on what good looks like, it becomes much easier to support growth, evaluate performance, and hire candidates who can succeed in the role.

Mehta frames the underlying philosophy this way: "peak" product managers don't just ship features — they deliver valuable outcomes for their users, their team, and their company. Most PMs, even strong ones, only excel at a handful of the twelve competencies; the difference between an average PM and a peak PM is understanding the gaps and building a team that fills them.

## How the model connects to performance and hiring

The model works best when used across the full employee lifecycle:

- competencies define expected behavior and outcomes
- performance reviews assess evidence of those competencies
- interview questions are designed to evaluate the same competencies

This alignment matters because if hiring interviews evaluate different signals than performance reviews, the organization creates confusion. Great hiring practices should reflect the actual work the role requires, and performance review standards should reflect the same underlying capabilities.

## The four competency categories

### 1. Product Execution — *Define, build, and launch exceptional products*

The foundation of product work: working with a cross-functional team to define, build, and launch well-designed, stable products.

- **Feature Specification** — the ability to gather requirements, define functionality, and set goals in a clear, actionable format that the team can use to drive delivery. A good spec doesn't need to be long — it needs to rally the team around a goal with enough detail to execute against. (Primary focus: APM through Director.)
- **Product Delivery** — the ability to work closely with the immediate team (engineering, design, etc.) to iteratively and quickly deliver product functionality against pre-defined goals. High-performing teams ship fast and iteratively, but stay honest by measuring against goals set up front. (Primary focus: PM through Director.)
- **Product Quality** (a.k.a. Quality Assurance) — the ability to identify, prioritize, and resolve technical, functional, and business quality issues across all devices, points of sale, and use cases. Quality rarely moves metrics in an obviously attributable way, which is exactly why it gets deprioritized — but poor quality slowly erodes trust and loyalty until a better competitor arrives. (Primary focus: APM and PM.)

### 2. Customer Insight — *Understand and fulfill customer needs*

Understanding customer needs and using evidence — both quantitative and qualitative — to make product decisions.

- **Fluency with Data** — the ability to use data to generate actionable insights, use those insights to hit product goals, and connect quantified goals to meaningful business outcomes. The best PMs go beyond reporting results — they dig for the causal relationships between user behavior and valuable outcomes. (Primary focus: PM through VP — one of the few competencies that stays important at every senior level.)
- **Voice of the Customer** — the ability to leverage user feedback in all its forms (casual conversations through formal studies) to understand engagement, make better decisions, and drive outcomes. Data describes behavior; only talking to customers explains it. As companies scale, informal customer channels tend to atrophy and get replaced by over-reliance on easy-to-access data — a common failure mode worth watching for. (Primary focus: PM through Sr. Director.)
- **User Experience Design** — the ability, individually and with the design team, to define requirements and deliver UX that is easy to use, follows UX best practices, and fits the product's existing patterns. The distinction that matters here is interaction design vs. visual design — clear labels usually beat "pretty" icons because they reduce friction. (Primary focus: Sr. PM and GPM.)

### 3. Product Strategy — *Deliver business impact via product innovation*

Aligns product work with business outcomes and long-term direction. Product Strategy is the glue joining product development to company strategy — good products must be valuable to both the customer *and* the business, or they end up in the "deadpool" of shut-down products that users loved but companies couldn't justify.

- **Business Outcome Ownership** — the ability to drive meaningful business outcomes by connecting product functionality and goals to the strategic objectives of the team and company. Mehta calls this the single most important competency, and the only one that matters equally **at every level**, from APM to CPO. Peak PMs seek out ownership of business results rather than shying away from it.
- **Product Vision & Roadmapping** — the ability to define an overall vision for a product area that connects to team and company strategy, plus a roadmap of prioritized features and initiatives that deliver against that vision. Early in a career this means shipping a good feature; later it means understanding how features ladder up into a cohesive vision. (Primary focus: Sr. PM through VP.)
- **Strategic Impact** — the ability to understand and contribute to business strategy for the team and company, and to bring strategy to fruition through consistent delivery of business outcomes. As PMs get senior, this extends to taking a seat at the table with other business leaders to help set strategy, not just execute it. (Primary focus: Sr. PM through VP.)

### 4. Influencing People — *Rally people around the team's work*

Peak PMs see themselves as connective tissue in the organization — leading through influence, not authority — and multiply their impact by enabling others.

- **Stakeholder Management** — the ability to proactively identify stakeholders impacted by the PM's area of ownership and factor their requirements into decisions. Stakeholders want to be included, not managed — but the job isn't to make everyone happy; compromise-driven products tend to be bloated and hard to use. (Primary focus: Sr. PM through VP.)
- **Team Leadership** — the ability to manage and mentor direct reports so they can deliver on their product areas, grow against these same competencies, deliver outcomes, and hit their own career goals. New PM leaders often over-rotate into micromanagement; peak PMs act as a coach first, reserving close management for underperformance. (Primary focus: GPM through VP.)
- **Managing Up** — the ability to leverage senior managers and executives to achieve goals, deliver outcomes, and positively influence team/company strategic direction. The core skill is alignment, not just communication: understand what your boss and other leaders are trying to achieve, and align your work to it — resources flow to the teams most aligned with leadership's objectives. (Primary focus: GPM through VP.)

## How competency importance shifts by level

Every competency matters at every level to some degree, but which ones differentiate a strong performer changes as a PM becomes more senior. The model tracks this as a clockwise progression, starting with Product Execution and ending with Influencing People:

| Level | Core focus |
| --- | --- |
| APM | Feature Specification, Product Quality — learning to execute; leans on the manager to help define *what* to build |
| PM | Feature Specification, Product Delivery, Product Quality, plus growing Fluency with Data and Voice of the Customer |
| Sr. PM | Shifts from feature-level execution into full ownership of Customer Insight (data, customer voice, UX) and the first real Product Strategy and Stakeholder Management responsibility |
| GPM | Customer Insight plus Product Strategy and Team Leadership — the "Product Manager → Product Leader" transition point |
| Director | Product Strategy and Influencing People become the primary differentiators; Product Execution matters far less as a personal skill |
| Sr. Director / VP | Strategic Impact, Product Vision & Roadmapping, Team Leadership, Managing Up dominate; hands-on execution and UX craft are no longer expected |

Two competencies are the exceptions to "importance changes by level":

- **Business Outcome Ownership** stays equally critical from APM to CPO.
- **Product Quality** and **Feature Specification** matter most early and are expected as a baseline (not a differentiator) later — a senior leader who has quietly stopped caring about quality is a red flag, not a sign of growth.

## Self-assessment rating scale

For quick, repeatable self- or manager-assessment (before a 1:1, review, or promotion decision), rate each of the 12 competencies on a three-tier scale:

- **Needs Focus**
- **On Track**
- **Outperform**

As a sanity check on a healthy profile: most PMs should land with **2–3 competencies "Outperform," 2–3 "Needs Focus," and the rest "On Track."** A profile with no "Needs Focus" ratings usually means the self-assessment isn't rigorous, not that the person is exceptional across the board — the model's core premise is that individuals are naturally "spiky," not well-rounded (see below).

## Knowing your shape

**Individuals should be spiky; teams should be well-rounded.** Research on high-performing companies (McKinsey/Egon Zehnder, "spiky leadership") found the best leaders are often average or deficient at several competencies — the outlier companies invested in people's strengths and tolerated weaknesses, rather than chasing well-roundedness. Incompetence is still subtractive and must be avoided, but excellence doesn't require covering every competency personally — it requires knowing your shape and pairing with people whose shape complements yours.

Two axes are useful for reading a person's (or a team's) shape:

**Product Builder vs. Product Architect** — Product Execution + Influencing People competencies make someone a *Builder* (drives the nails); Customer Insight + Product Strategy competencies make someone an *Architect* (drafts the blueprints). Builders need to grow Customer Insight and Product Strategy to keep advancing; Architects should invest further in their strengths and pair with strong Builders to bring ideas to life.

**Product Manager vs. Product Leader** — Product Execution + Customer Insight are the "Product Manager" hemisphere; Product Strategy + Influencing People are the "Product Leader" hemisphere. The natural growth path moves clockwise: master execution first, then customer insight at the feature level, then vision/strategy across features, then influence at organizational scale. Business Outcome Ownership is the one competency that anchors the whole journey regardless of hemisphere.

Use these axes when building or evaluating a team: a team that is lopsided toward Builders will ship the wrong things well; a team that is all Architects will have great ideas nobody executes. Fill spiky-people gaps either by pairing complementary PMs, or by leaning on strong cross-functional partners (analytics, design, engineering) rather than forcing one person to cover every competency.

## Common PM archetypes

These are intentionally exaggerated patterns for spotting growth opportunities in yourself or a report — not boxes to permanently file someone into.

- **The Project Manager** — strong Product Execution and Stakeholder Management; weak Customer Insight and Product Strategy. Great at shipping, not yet equipped to know *what* to ship. Growth path: build Customer Insight and Product Strategy skills before further promotion, even with a strong shipping track record.
- **The People Manager** — strong Influencing People (especially Stakeholder Management, Managing Up); weaker Customer Insight and Product Strategy. Rallies teams and leadership effectively, but risks leading them toward the wrong destination. Growth path: invest in Customer Insight and Product Strategy so leadership skill has the right target.
- **The Growth Hacker** — strong Product Execution, Fluency with Data, and Business Outcome Ownership; weaker Voice of the Customer and Product Vision & Roadmapping. Excellent at optimizing the existing product (local maxima); risks confusing growth optimization with product innovation and missing disruption (global maxima). Growth path: reinforce data/outcome strengths further, *or* deliberately build Voice of the Customer and Vision & Roadmapping.
- **The Product Innovator** — strong Voice of the Customer, User Experience Design, and Product Vision & Roadmapping; weaker Product Delivery and Fluency with Data. Excellent at sensing what's next and painting a compelling vision; risks over-investing in big, unvalidated bets because ideas aren't broken into iterative, measurable pieces. Growth path: pair with execution/data-driven teammates, or personally build up Product Delivery and Fluency with Data.

## Practical applications

- **Evaluate yourself.** Rate all 12 competencies Needs Focus / On Track / Outperform to find strengths, gaps, and a growth plan. Expect 2–3 Outperform and 2–3 Needs Focus.
- **Supercharge a 1:1.** Have both the report and the manager rate all 12 independently before the meeting, then compare. Leave the 1:1 with one strength to double down on and one gap to focus on — this beats a generic "how's it going" 1:1.
- **Hire better PMs.** A 1–2 round loop can't realistically probe all 12 competencies well. Assign each interviewer one of the four categories (Execution, Insight, Strategy, Influence) so the panel collectively covers the model, and standardize questions per category across interviewers for calibration.
- **Make smarter promotion decisions.** Rate the candidate across all 12 before deciding. They should be "Outperform" on most competencies for the next level, with no critical deficits. Whatever the decision, share the ratings — as the reason for a "not yet," or as the expectations that come with a "yes."
- **Build more balanced teams.** Use an individual's shape (see above) to decide who to pair them with, or which cross-functional partner (analytics, design, engineering) should fill a known gap, rather than expecting one PM to be strong everywhere.

## Model use guidance

The competency model should be used as a practical tool for:

- evaluating strengths and gaps
- discussing development opportunities
- setting expectations for role progression
- guiding interview rubrics and calibration sessions

When used well, the model reduces bias, improves consistency, and gives product leaders a clearer way to support both performance and growth.

## Important principle

A competency model is most valuable when it is used consistently. If a manager uses one standard in performance reviews and a different standard in hiring, the result is misalignment. The recommended approach is to ensure that interview questions, evaluation rubrics, and development discussions all map back to the same competency framework — and, where useful, the same Needs Focus / On Track / Outperform scale.
